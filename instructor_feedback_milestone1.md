# Instructor Feedback on Milestone 1

## Collective Feedback on Topic, Scholarly Context, and Collaboration Plan

I really like your group theme of sports becoming part of national and global culture through television and digital media. I think athlete mobility, international NBA players, and national allegiance in Formula 1 give you distinct projects that can still speak to each other. You might further narrow the framing of your theme through a temporal focus, maybe a particular decade or just the 21st century, but indicating the focus will help future researchers understand the scope of your work.

I also really appreciated your scholarly context, especially that you explained how each source relates to the proposed work rather than just listing citations. Your use of *Sport beyond television* also gives you a useful shared framework. I think there is an opportunity here to ask not just how media make sports visible, but whose interests that visibility serves. Leagues, clubs, broadcasters, and platforms may have different reasons for promoting particular athletes or national narratives, and those interests might shape the cultural data you find.

Your methodological and ethical sections are also promising. I particularly like your recognition that nationality and fan identity are not neutral categories, and that one source might contain competing frames. As you build your datasets, I would like to see those commitments reflected in the actual fields: where will you preserve an example, explain a classification, or record uncertainty? The scholarship could help you decide which categories to adopt, adapt, or question.

The main piece that needs revision is turning each individual topic into a concrete plan for both approaches to culture as data. Bob has started to describe a custom dataset, but I still need each of you to identify what your initial 50-100 items will be, what existing data you will audit, and how those two parts connect. You should also explain where computation will assist your work beyond entering information into a spreadsheet. You do not need to have solved every technical question yet, but I need to see a feasible starting point.

I think your collaboration plan sounds sensible, and I appreciate the use of GitHub Issues or a project board. Your next step is to make that plan more specific: decide where individual data, code, documentation, and collective website materials will live, and how you will divide and review the website work. Please also use your group logs and commits to make everyone's contributions visible.

Overall, I think you have a strong shared topic and scholarly foundation, so looking forward to seeing how you turn that into a concrete plan for your datasets and website.

## Individual Feedback

### Mason

I like your interest in athlete mobility and opportunity, especially the question of why moving to Europe might become part of an athlete's career story. Your use of Velema gives you a useful way to situate that question within recruitment networks and unequal opportunities, rather than treating relocation as simply an individual choice.

I think your next step though is to decide what one row in your custom dataset represents. Is it an athlete, a career move, or a public account of that move? You might focus on a particular group of players or time period and create records from biographies, media profiles, or published interviews. Your interpretive fields could capture how those sources describe opportunity, prestige, barriers, or support. The initial 50-100 items do not have to mean 50-100 athletes if you are working with multiple accounts or career transitions.

You also need to consider how computation will play a role in your work. You could use Python to extract names or dates from public materials, organize career timelines, or check consistency across records, while explaining the choices you make about what counts as a move or an opportunity. I would be careful about claiming to know an athlete's motivations unless you have evidence for that interpretation. Published interviews could help, but again you want to think critically about what those sources reveal and what they leave out. You might end up web scraping to collect these sources, or perhaps a website related to a particular league or team to find player profiles. Please explain what you will use and how you will preserve the evidence for each record. If you decide to do mapping, I would highly recommend taking a look at the Python Folium library [https://folium.readthedocs.io/en/latest/](https://folium.readthedocs.io/en/latest/), which is a great way to visualize geographic data.

You also need to identify the existing data you will audit. A football transfer or player-mobility dataset might be a natural comparison, but you need to choose a specific source and explain how you will connect its records to your custom work. What does a transfer record tell us, and what does it leave out about the experience or circumstances of moving? That difference could become a really interesting part of your project, though you'll need to think about how you can verify the existing records and consider what they leave out. The existing dataset might also help you think about what that source actually counts as mobility. A recorded transfer between clubs is not necessarily the same thing as relocating for training or growing up in another country. So you could not only investigate the dataset's coverage, but how it records dates and locations, and which kinds of movement are absent. Then you could compare to your custom dataset to see how different approaches exist for capturing mobility. You might also consider the politics of immigration, and how that shapes who can go where.

In terms of scholarly context, I think you have a strong start with Velema, but you might also look for scholarship on athlete migration or the cultural prestige of particular leagues to help distinguish institutional opportunities from the narratives surrounding them. You could also consider how media coverage of athletes' moves might shape perceptions of opportunity and prestige, and how that interacts with the structural factors you are studying. Overall, I think you have an interesting question, and narrowing your focus, whether through the players, period, or kinds of movement, will help make the project feasible. Happy to discuss potential starting points if you want to brainstorm together.

### Drew

I really like your interest in international NBA players as a connection between local identities and a global league. The comparison between U.S. coverage and coverage associated with a player's home country seems especially promising, as well as your list of identified players.

I think the next step will be starting to think about how to track this coverage and whether you will focus on all four players listed or narrow to one or two. You might also consider a particular time period, such as the 2020-2021 season, or a specific event, like the NBA Finals or an All-Star Game. Generally, you'll need to decide what one row in your custom dataset represents: a news article, a broadcast segment, or another type of cultural item. 

This might be a chance to also consider how you plan to bring in computation. You could web scrapte news coverage from a manageable set of outlets and social media platforms, and explain how you will select those items. Alternatively, you could create a data entry interface to categorize media coverage, looking at how players are described, which identities are emphasized, and whether they are framed as local heroes, international stars, or something else. You might even experiment with how to operationalize your categories computationally, such as using text analysis to identify recurring terms or phrases that maybe you've first identified through close reading. I would also encourage you to distinguish between asking how media represent international players and claiming that media caused the NBA's international growth. The latter would require evidence beyond the fact that coverage and international participation both exist. Similarly, birthplace, citizenship, national-team affiliation, and fan identity should not automatically become the same category in your dataset.

You also need to identify the existing dataset you will audit. You might consider roster data, broadcast or viewership records, or platform engagement data, depending on what is accessible. But I would like you to choose a specific source and explain what it measures. How will those records connect to the players or coverage in your custom dataset, and what might a numerical measure of visibility miss about cultural influence? I think that would help make the two approaches distinct but connected. You should also think about how you will verify the existing records and handle the period they represent. If you choose video or broadcasts, consider what materials or transcripts you can actually access before making automated collection central to the plan.

Finally, I think the Falcous and Maguire piece is useful, but it is a bit older (2006), so you might take a look at more recent research that cites it to see how newer scholarship is approaching this question of international media coverage of the NBA. I also think the ethical questions your group raises about national identity, as well as the Slater, Burch, and Zimmerman piece will be useful to you. Overall, I think there is a strong project here if you focus on how particular players are represented rather than trying to explain the NBA's entire globalization. Happy to discuss more if you want to brainstorm potential starting points or sources for your custom dataset.
